Guides · Maps and data

Why your maps don’t line up, and which one to trust.

You lay a floor plan or a site map over Google Maps and the building sits fifteen feet off, in the parking lot. Usually neither map is simply “wrong.” Here’s why maps disagree, and how to tell which one to rely on for the decision in front of you.

Last reviewed November 2026 · For property managers, owners and facilities teams

The short version

Photos aren’t maps
An aerial or satellite photo looks like a map, but tall things lean in it, and the correction is never perfect.
Free basemaps vary
Google, Bing and Apple don’t publish how accurate their imagery is, and it changes from one block to the next.
Agreement proves little
Two layers that line up may just share the same mistake.
Right isn’t one thing
A plan can be perfect inside and still sit in the wrong spot on the earth.
Before you dig
Call 811, then find your private lines too. No map alone is ever enough.

A photo from the air leans

A camera in an airplane or satellite sees most of the ground at an angle, not straight down. Anything with height, like a building, a tree or a light pole, appears to lean away from the center of the picture. On a three-story office, the roof edge in the photo can sit several feet off from where the walls meet the ground, and more on taller buildings.

Imagery providers straighten photos out using elevation data and known points on the ground. The result is called an orthophoto, and it’s much closer to a true map. But it’s only as good as the elevation data and ground points behind it, and rooftops often still don’t sit exactly over their foundations.

Why it matters to you: if someone traced your building outlines from the roofs in a photo, the outlines may be shifted by the same amount, and nothing on screen will warn you.

Free basemaps are a patchwork

The imagery behind Google Maps, Bing Maps and Apple Maps is stitched together from many flights and satellites, taken in different years by different companies. In one place it can be within a few feet of true. A mile away it can be off by ten, twenty or more. The companies don’t publish accuracy figures for it, so there’s no way to know how good a given spot is without checking it against something you trust.

That makes free imagery great for orientation, the “which building is which” kind of question. It’s a poor yardstick for deciding whether your own map is right.

Other reasons two maps disagree

  • Different dates. The photo is from before the addition was built, or the parking lot was restriped since.
  • Different reference points. Every survey or photo flight is tied to its own set of known points on the ground. Different jobs, slightly different results.
  • Different versions of the coordinate system. The systems that turn coordinates into places on the ground have been updated over the years. The same numbers can land a foot or more apart depending on which version was used.
  • Drawings that were never tied to the ground. Many floor plans and site plans have no coordinates at all. Someone has to fit them onto the map, and the fit is only as good as what they lined them up with.

The trap is assuming that when two layers line up, both are right. If one was traced from the other, or both came from the same photo, they’ll agree perfectly and share the same error.

Right inside, wrong on the earth

This one surprises people. A floor plan can be drawn beautifully: every room the right size, every hallway the right width, every door where it belongs. Then someone fits it onto the map by lining it up with a free satellite image that’s twenty feet off. Now the whole building is twenty feet off, too, while every measurement inside it is still correct.

GIS people call this the difference between precision, how well the shapes match each other, and accuracy, how well they match the real ground. A plan like that is still excellent for:

  • knowing which room is which, and sending a technician to the right door;
  • square footage and room layouts;
  • pinning repairs to the rooms where they happen.

It is not good for deciding where a wall sits relative to a buried line, a setback or a property boundary. Those questions are about the ground, so they need data that’s accurate on the ground.

Match the accuracy to the decision

“How accurate is the map?” matters less than “is it accurate enough for what I’m about to decide?” A rough guide:

Which building or unit?
Almost any decent map, free imagery included.
Rooms, square feet, work orders
A floor plan that’s right inside. Where it sits on the earth matters much less.
Walking to a shut-off or cleanout
Features located in the field, by GPS or measured from the building, close enough that someone can find them in the dark.
Property lines, setbacks, construction
A licensed land surveyor. Maps, ours included, are not a substitute.
Digging
811 for the utilities’ lines, a private locator for yours, and expose the line by hand before machines get close.

For mapping professionals, the formal yardstick is the American Society for Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing’s positional accuracy standards. A good provider can tell you how their work measures up.

When someone says your map is wrong

Ask what they compared it to. A phone’s GPS can easily be off by tens of feet, especially near buildings and under trees. A free basemap has no published accuracy at all. A contractor with survey-grade equipment and a clear discrepancy is worth listening to. Then check where your layer came from. Which brings us to the most useful habit of all.

Good maps say where they came from

For every layer you rely on, you should be able to find out:

  • What it was made from: a survey, field GPS, a drawing, or tracing a photo?
  • How it was placed on the ground, and what it was lined up with.
  • About how accurate it is, in feet.
  • When someone last checked it against the real thing.

If nobody can answer those questions, treat the layer as approximate until someone verifies it.

Before anyone digs

Maps of underground utilities show where lines are thought to be, based on drawings and records. Lines get moved, abandoned or installed without paperwork, and an error that looks tiny on screen is big enough to hit a gas line. In Georgia, the law requires contacting Georgia 811 before any mechanized digging. You call 811 or file a request online at least 48 hours, and no more than 10 working days, before you start, and the utilities mark their lines. It’s worth doing for hand digging too.

But 811 isn’t the whole answer. The utilities that respond mark only their own public lines, generally up to the meter or service point. Everything past that is private and the property owner’s responsibility: the service lines into buildings, irrigation, site lighting, lines to outbuildings, propane, wells, and on campuses and larger properties, entire private water, sewer, power and data systems. Those need a private utility locator.

Even a good locator can miss a line. Locators find most lines by putting a signal on metal. Plastic, clay and concrete pipe give them nothing to follow unless a tracer wire or detectable tape was buried with it, and that wire is often missing, broken or corroded. Ground-penetrating radar helps in some soils but struggles in wet clay, and north Georgia has plenty of that. That’s why first-hand knowledge matters: the maintenance tech who was there when the line went in, an as-built drawing someone actually checked, and careful hand digging or vacuum excavation to expose the line before machines get close.

If you own private lines, the cheapest locate you’ll ever get is the one done while the trench is open: when a line is exposed for a repair or a new install, get it mapped before it’s buried again.

If your organization has records scattered across old drawings and people’s memories, or a crew just uncovered a line nobody knew was there, give us a call. We can help get it documented in your systems while the information is fresh.

This guide is a plain-language overview. It isn’t surveying, engineering or legal advice. For property lines and construction layout, use a licensed land surveyor; for buried utilities, use 811 and a private utility locator.
